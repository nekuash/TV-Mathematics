Channel 01 · The Screen

okay so, the TV itself is already math

Before any show even plays, I realized the TV as an object is built entirely out of numbers. Here's what I noticed when I actually looked into it.

CH 01

wait, the "size" isn't the width?

I used to assume a "55-inch" TV meant it was 55 inches across. Turns out that number is the diagonal, corner to corner. Once I thought about it as a triangle (width, height, and the diagonal as the hypotenuse), the Pythagorean theorem showed up. just sitting inside a TV spec sheet.

55" diagonal
CH 01

why do old shows have black bars?

This one bugged me for a while. It comes down to aspect ratio, basically a ratio between width and height, like 16:9 for modern screens versus 4:3 for older ones. When the ratios don't match, the TV pads the difference with black bars instead of stretching the image weirdly.

16:9 widescreen
CH 01

4K isn't "double" HD, and that caught me out

I assumed 4K meant twice the detail of Full HD. Not quite, resolution is just width times height in pixels, and since both dimensions doubled, the actual pixel count is four times as many, not two. Simple multiplication, but it changed how I read spec sheets.

1920 × 1080 px
CH 01

16.7 million colors, from just three numbers

This is the one that made me appreciate exponents outside of a textbook. Each color channel (red, green, blue) has 256 possible values, that's 2⁸. Multiply the three channels together and you get every color the screen can actually show.

256 × 256 × 256
Channel 02 · The Signal

getting the picture to actually reach you

This part surprised me the most, a lot of "invisible" TV stuff is really just math working fast enough that you never catch it happening.

CH 02

60Hz just means "60 times a second"

I always saw "60Hz" on TVs and shrugged past it. It's just frequency, how many times the screen redraws the image every second. Higher number, smoother motion. Same concept as frequency in physics, just applied somewhere I didn't expect.

CH 02

frequency and wavelength fight each other

TV signals travel as electromagnetic waves, and here's the part I had to sit with: frequency and wavelength are inversely related. Push the frequency up, the wavelength shrinks. That relationship is basically the whole reason broadcast math exists.

CH 02

why streaming buffers (it's a math comparison, not "bad wifi")

I always blamed my internet when something buffered, but it's really a comparison happening in real time. Compression math shrinks the video down first, based on a compression ratio. Then, while streaming, the player is constantly checking the bitrate the video needs against the bandwidth your connection can actually deliver. The second bandwidth loses that race, buffering kicks in.

CH 02

your remote speaks in 1s and 0s

Every button press sends a pattern of infrared pulses that's really just binary, a sequence of 1s and 0s the TV reads and decodes. I didn't expect binary, of all things, to be sitting inside something as ordinary as a remote.

CH 02

volume doesn't go up in a straight line

This one felt counterintuitive: going from volume 20 to 40 isn't "twice as loud." Decibels follow a logarithmic scale, not a linear one, so the jump in perceived loudness is much bigger than the numbers suggest.

Channel 03 · The Viewer

turns out I'm doing math too, just by watching

This is the part I almost left out, because it felt like a stretch at first, but math doesn't stop at the screen. My own eyes and brain are quietly doing geometry the whole time I'm watching.

CH 03

there's an actual "correct" distance to sit

I thought where I sat was just personal preference. Apparently standards groups like SMPTE and THX have real formulas connecting screen size to recommended viewing distance, so your eyes take in the full picture comfortably. Sit too close or too far and the geometry between your eyes and the screen just stops working the way it's supposed to.

CH 03

my eyes can't actually see the pixels

From a normal couch distance, individual pixels blend together into a smooth image instead of a visible grid. How close I'd need to sit before that breaks down is, itself, a geometry problem, angles and distance deciding what my eyes can resolve.

CH 03

brightness plays the same trick as volume

Same logic as the decibel thing earlier, screen brightness isn't tuned on a flat 1-to-1 scale either. It follows a curve built around how human eyes actually perceive light, not how the raw numbers look on paper.

Channel 04 · Sign Off

so yeah, it really does all add up

Screen size, resolution, color, signal, compression, seating distance, even volume, none of it turned out to be random. Once I started looking, I couldn't find a single part of "just watching TV" that wasn't running on math somewhere underneath.
